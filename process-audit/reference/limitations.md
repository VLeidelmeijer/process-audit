Read this before reporting. Most of what follows is a limit on **wording**, and wording is where an
unfounded claim actually enters — not in the analysis.

## What this skill may claim about itself

**Nothing beyond "observed on these cases".**

The instrument is unvalidated. In the material it was built from there is no independent rater
reliability, no demonstrated transfer between process types, no demonstrated causal improvement, no
measured net benefit of its three mechanisms, and no inventory of the artifacts it will actually be
used on. That is not a gap waiting to be filled by more reading; it is the state of the evidence.

The claim ladder, in order. Nothing higher may be stated without the evidence named beside it:

| Claim | What it needs |
|---|---|
| "observed on these cases" | The cases, and their outcomes |
| "repeatable for this assessor" | A blinded re-rate on frozen cases — and this is the highest claim currently reachable |
| "agrees with an independent criterion" | A criterion genuinely external to this instrument |
| Anything broader | Evidence this instrument does not have |

**No reliability claim of any kind, and no fixture pass rate reported as one.** On a set of sixteen
cases a clean sweep licenses no reliability language at all: eighteen of twenty passing is 90%
observed with a 95% interval running roughly 70% to 97%, which is a calculation and not a threshold.
A zero count on known critical negative cases supports a **gate**; it supports no inference from
having passed it.

**Repeatability is not correctness.** A lone assessor can be perfectly self-consistent and
systematically wrong. Self-consistency is not inter-rater agreement and is not evidence of accuracy.

## The sentence that goes in every report using judgement

> Inter-human rater reliability was not estimated.

It is not a caveat to add if there is room. It is what stops a repeatability figure being read as
the thing it is not.

## Numbers that were not located

Every one of these is a number a report would find convenient. None was located in the material
this skill was built from, and inventing one is the most predictable way this instrument goes wrong.
Each line below is bounded that way: it records what that material did not supply, not a proof that
no such number exists.

- **No evidence-based universal criterion count**, for any checklist or rubric.
- **No function converting "remove one criterion" into expected additional missed failures.** There
  is no marginal-detection curve to argue from.
- **No defensible fixed number of response categories.** The literature conflicts, and one study
  across two to nineteen alternatives found no orderly relationship at all between the number of
  categories and reliability or validity.
- **No universal shelf life for a conclusion**, and no located empirical calibration for the
  certification intervals that do exist in published standards.
- **No transferable assessment time budget, and no stable assessment-to-production ratio** —
  including for AI-assisted and agentic work, where the question was asked directly and left
  unknown rather than filled with a conventional percentage.
- **No validated universal review percentage**, and no fixed quota for sentinel or untargeted
  checking. A coverage floor, not a number.
- **No validated universal severity formula**, and no numeric threshold for calling a cause
  "systemic" from its breadth.
- **No universal minimum run count for a stochastic process.**
- **No universal development/test split ratio.** The separation principle is far better supported
  than any percentage of it.
- **No universal decisive-content survival rate for summarisation** — and specifically none for
  the categories that matter most here: qualifiers, exceptions, caveats, negation, contradictions
  and numeric precision.
- **Practitioner sample-size heuristics of 30, 60 and 100 cases are operational advice, not
  psychometric constants.**

## Superiority claims that are not established

- **No general winner between binary and graded scoring.** The one head-to-head measurement located
  went *against* checklists: expert global ratings showed higher inter-station reliability and
  better construct and concurrent validity in a surgical-skills examination. This skill is binary
  anyway — for a different reason, that its graded alternative could not yet satisfy its own
  admission condition — and not because binary was shown to be better.
- **No general winner between broad-shallow, narrow-deep and pure risk targeting.**
- **No controlled comparison of taxonomy-first against observed-failure-first category
  generation was located.** Bottom-up derivation is promising and well motivated operationally; it
  is not experimentally shown to dominate, and it has a real structural weakness — it cannot encode
  an important failure that has not appeared in the observed sample yet.
- **No validation of developmental rubric levels as causal improvement steps was located.** Three
  propositions get conflated here and only the weakest is usually evidenced: that the descriptions
  form an ordered set; that higher scorers tend to have better outcomes; and that performing a
  level's prescribed actions *causes* movement to the next.
- **Universal over-trust of a single score is disconfirmed** as a general claim — three experiments
  totalling 2,854 participants found no generalised automation bias against equivalent human advice
  when contradictory evidence was available. And requested subscores frequently add no reliable
  information beyond a total. Neither result refutes the design here; both remove the usual
  justification for it. **A richer record cannot be claimed to be a better-read record.**
- **"An automated judge always prefers longer answers" is not supportable as a general law**, and
  apparent self-preference largely does not survive a better statistical null.

## Things that cannot be estimated from what an audit has

- **The rate at which `not applicable` is abused.** No defensible empirical estimate was located, so
  mandatory rationales and rate monitoring must not be presented as interventions proven to reduce
  it. They are monitorable, not quantified.
- **How often a lone technical assessor misuses `not applicable` as an escape hatch.** Plausible
  and worth watching; not measured.
- **Whether readers act on evidence-basis qualifications.** Studies measure interpretation and
  media transmission, not decisions.
- **How often post-closure reopening or revocation actually happens.** The mechanism is
  operationally specified; that it is consistently exercised is not shown.
- **The defect-catching yield of generic source-to-target reconciliation**, and the return on
  lineage instrumentation. Note the refusal in both directions: no evidence was located that heavy
  reconciliation routinely catches nothing either, and null results in this area may be
  under-reported.
- **Multiplying per-step accuracies is not a valid estimator of chain success.**
- **A formal value-of-information calculation is only as good as the probabilities and costs put
  into it.** A solo assessor typically lacks both, so pseudo-precise scores built on guesses have no
  support.

## Claims that look strong and do not transfer

The source dossier names fourteen claim families where a strong-looking label must not be carried
across into a precise gate, an effect claim or a domain transfer: normative property bases,
benchmark numbers, change-metric definitions, maturity ladders, sample sizes, architecture
heuristics, judge reliability, evidence levels, data contracts, security heuristics, documentation
practice, solo instrumentation, determinism, and specification quality. Every criterion here that
touches one of those carries the limit in its own entry.

Two general rules behind that list:

- **A high formal assurance grade can accompany a wrong conclusion.** Products certified at high
  assurance levels have been found with critical vulnerabilities, and safety-case adoption across
  several industries was not itself based on empirical evidence that safety cases improve safety.
- **Automation does not raise the grade.** An automated oracle provides highly repeatable evidence —
  for whatever proposition it actually tests. Repeatability is not validity.

Three specific non-transfers worth naming, because each is a number a report would reach for:

- **Clinical decay figures do not transfer** to software, procedures, scripts, prompts or human
  processes. The heterogeneity inside that one discipline is itself the argument.
- **Hand-hygiene observer-effect magnitudes do not transfer** to code review, procedure compliance,
  pipeline operation or agentic work. Hand hygiene is unusually observable, repetitive and
  norm-laden.
- **Benchmark numbers in the underlying reports are not audit thresholds.** The source dossier
  forbids carrying them across, explicitly.

## What a single assessor cannot do, and why it is not a gap

**One human assessor cannot estimate inter-rater reliability among humans.** With one rater the
rater facet is not observed. This is a definitional boundary, not an evidence gap — no amount of
solo re-rating crosses it, and no future study will.

Re-rating your own retained cases is still worth doing. It measures whether *you* reproduce *your
own* judgements on a changed occasion. It omits between-person differences in how a criterion is
interpreted, in severity, in scale use and in attention.

**How to run the blinded re-rate.** It is the only route to "repeatable for this assessor" on the
claim ladder, and it is the drift check below, so it runs to a protocol written down before its
first session:

- **Freeze the cases**, and the version of this skill they are rated against. Name each case and
  fix the material it is rated on; both sessions rate that material and nothing added between them.
- **Conceal the earlier verdicts and their rationales.** The second session sees neither the values
  the first gave nor the reasons written for them — not as a report, a note or a summary.
- **Change the case order.** The second session takes the cases in a different order, fixed before
  it starts.
- **Carry no prior score into the second session.** It starts from each case's frozen material
  alone.
- **Decide beforehand what a genuinely changed case does.** A case whose material has to change
  between the sessions leaves the comparison and is reported as having left; it is not re-rated
  against its old values.
- **State the interval and its reason.** No cross-domain minimum interval was located in the
  material this skill was built from, so no number is borrowed: the reason is local — what the
  interval has to let fade, and what must stay fixed across it.
- **Record the result:** for each case and criterion, both values, and every disagreement listed.

**What the result reports.** Raw agreement and the count of each value in each session, beside any
chance-corrected coefficient and never replaced by one; agreement on `does not meet`, the value this
instrument exists to catch, reported on its own; never a correlation as an agreement statistic; and
the distribution of the cases named, by type and by value — so that a figure carried by cases that
came out `meets` both times is visible as such.

**Six critical perspectives applied by one model are not an independent rater design.** A simulated
persona critique yields counter-questions and design refinements, not evidence. Calibration is not
a substitute for independence, and describing yourself as both the instrument's author and its
independent expert reviewer collapses two roles into one.

**An AI is a second procedure, never a second human rater.** The correct quantity is human-versus-AI
agreement for a named model or version, prompt, scale and case distribution. And there is a measured
trap specific to this configuration: anchoring an AI to a supplied prior judgement blocked a large
share of error corrections and flipped correct categorical judgements toward the supplied wrong
label, in seven of eight models tested — and **neither chain-of-thought prompting nor an instruction
to disregard the anchor removed the effect.** Do not show it your verdict. Do not use "the AI agreed
with me" as validation when its prompt or examples were tuned against the same labels.

**Assessment also changes the assessor.** Repeated exposure can shift thresholds, attention and
expectations with no independent reviewer present to notice the drift. Recalibrate the assessor
periodically, not only the assessed process — the blinded re-rate does that job as well as its
first one.

## Why this instrument is process-type-agnostic

An earlier attempt at this failed because its criteria were fitted to a single case and transferred
nowhere else. That history is carried here as the reason the transfer question matters — not as an
enforcement mechanism. How to guard against overfitting is an open method question, and the
core-plus-module architecture is a response to it, not a solution.

Validity attaches to a particular interpretation and use, never to an instrument in the abstract.
A new object class is therefore a new use, and its first assessments are marked provisional — and
stay so until the condition in `SKILL.md` Step 0 holds. A lone practitioner assessing a small number
of heterogeneous systems will not have the sample size to demonstrate statistical invariance, and in
that situation the evidence does **not** license assuming it.

## Cases, and the one-way ratchet on them

Development cases and evaluation cases are separate pools, and the separation decays under use
unless a one-way rule protects it:

> **Any case used to modify the instrument is spent.** It moves to the development pool and must be
> replaced before the next evaluation.

You cannot repair a criterion in response to a case and still call that case's score part of a final
unbiased estimate. After such a repair it is a development case, permanently.

The deeper risk is not "too few cases". It is **dependence between how the rules were created and
how the supposed ground truth was established.** Fifty balanced cases whose expected answers were
inferred from the same rubric provide less external validity evidence than ten cases with genuinely
independent outcomes. Build the evaluation pool from held-out natural cases, known negatives and
counter-cases, seeded faults of known class, genuine `not applicable` cases, genuine
`not established` cases, and real historical failures.

Repeated runs on the same cases do not produce new independent situations, however many times they
are run.

### Evaluation pool — provenance and known limits

- **Provenance deviates from the protocol.** The practitioner does not have enough
  projects, incident records or retained test results of their own to fill the required
  mix of kinds and process types. The pool was therefore assembled by a session on a
  different model family (GPT), from public incident reports and documentation plus
  constructed examples, instead of from material the practitioner supplied. That session
  never saw the instrument. A post-delivery review session (Claude, which had not read the
  instrument) made limited, logged corrections. The answer keys are kept outside the
  repository.
- **Most cases are constructed.** Their facts are true by construction, like a planted
  fault, rather than observed in a real process.
- **Recognisability.** Cases derived from well-known public incident reports stayed
  identifiable to a capable model in a blinded test, even after organisations, dates and
  figures were changed. An auditor may bring in knowledge from outside the supplied
  evidence. The expected-outcome mapping deals with this.
- **Closed-world statements.** Some packets state their own boundaries ("complete
  evidence", "no production connection"). These are claims made inside the evidence;
  the mapping fixes in advance how they count.
- **Test-material markers.** Names carry "Fictional" / `FIC-` markers, so an auditor can
  tell the material is a test.
- **Model substitutes.** Processes built on LLMs were run with deterministic model
  substitutes, not live models.
